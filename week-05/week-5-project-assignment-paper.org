* Executive Summary

Orion Manufacturing sold 12,000 units, 2,000 more than the 10,000
units in its static budget. That volume growth produced a favorable
sales activity variance of *$64,000* in operating income. However,
actual operating income was only *$115,600*, compared with *$125,000*
in the static budget and *$189,000* in the flexible budget at 12,000
units.  Orion therefore missed the flexible-budget target by *$73,400
unfavorable*. The largest problems were an unfavorable sales price
variance of *$24,000*, unfavorable variable-cost flexible-budget
variances totaling *$44,400*, and an unfavorable fixed manufacturing
overhead variance of *$8,000*, partly offset by a favorable fixed
selling and administrative variance of *$3,000*.

The quarter should not be considered fully successful. Sales growth is
a genuine strength, but the static-budget-only comparison used by the
CEO is misleading because it does not adjust variable costs for the
20% increase in volume. At the same time, the production manager
cannot explain away all unfavorable results: per-unit direct
materials, direct labor, variable manufacturing overhead, and variable
selling expenses all exceeded budget. Orion needs flexible-budget
reporting, deeper variance investigation, stronger cost
accountability, and nonfinancial performance measures to protect
long-term strategy.

* Introduction

Orion Manufacturing produces a specialized commercial air-filtration
unit. Management prepared a static budget for 10,000 units, but actual
production and sales reached 12,000 units. The CEO concluded that
costs were poorly controlled because most actual expenses exceeded the
static budget. The production manager argued that the comparison is
unfair because activity was 20% higher than planned. This report
evaluates both positions using static and flexible budget analysis. It
separates activity-driven variances from operating-performance
variances, assesses managerial responsibility, and recommends actions
for cost control and future performance evaluation.

Although the framing question posits a scenario in which operating
income exceeded the static-budget target but fell below the
flexible-budget target, Orion's actual results show operating income
below both targets. This makes the performance concern even stronger
and reinforces the need to distinguish volume effects from
controllable operating performance.

* Static and Flexible Budget Concepts

A static budget is prepared for one planned activity level and is
useful for initial planning, resource allocation, and setting baseline
targets (Hansen & Van der Stede, 2004). It does not change when actual
volume differs from plan. A flexible budget, by contrast, adjusts
revenues and variable costs to the actual activity level. It is
therefore more useful for control and performance evaluation because
it compares actual results with what costs and revenues should have
been at the actual volume achieved (Libby & Lindsay, 2010).

The difference between the static and flexible budgets is the *sales
activity variance*. The difference between actual results and the
flexible budget is the *flexible-budget variance*, which reflects
price, spending, and efficiency performance. Together, the sales
activity variance and flexible-budget variance explain the total
static-budget variance. This separation is essential for
responsibility accounting because managers should generally be
evaluated on revenues and costs they can influence, not on
volume-driven changes outside their control (Hansen & Van der Stede,
2004; Merchant, 1985).

The flexible-budget variances show that Orion's per-unit operating
performance was weak. Actual direct materials cost $26 per unit versus
the $24 standard, creating a $24,000 unfavorable variance. Direct
labor was $16 per unit versus $15, for a $12,000 unfavorable variance.
Variable manufacturing overhead was $6.50 per unit versus $6, for a
$6,000 unfavorable variance. Variable selling expense was $3.20 per
unit versus $3, for a $2,400 unfavorable variance. Sales price was $78
per unit instead of $80, producing a $24,000 unfavorable revenue
variance.

* Evaluation of Managerial Performance

The CEO's conclusion is partly correct but incomplete. Comparing
actual variable costs only with the static budget unfairly penalizes
managers for volume growth. For example, direct materials were $72,000
above static budget, but $48,000 of that difference is activity-driven
and should be expected at 12,000 units. The remaining $24,000 is a
genuine operating-performance problem. Similarly, direct labor was
$42,000 above static budget, but $30,000 is activity-driven and
$12,000 is unfavorable flexible-budget performance.

The production manager is most likely accountable for direct
materials, direct labor, and variable manufacturing overhead
flexible-budget variances totaling *$42,000 unfavorable* ($24,000 +
$12,000 + $6,000).  These variances suggest excessive usage, higher
prices, inefficiencies, or outdated standards. Variable selling
expense of $2,400 unfavorable may be shared with sales management. The
$24,000 unfavorable sales price variance is primarily a sales and
marketing issue, not a production issue. Fixed manufacturing overhead
was $8,000 over budget, which may reflect capacity, maintenance,
overtime, or spending decisions that require further investigation.

Overall, the quarter was not fully successful. The 2,000-unit sales
increase generated $64,000 of favorable activity-driven operating
income, but $73,400 of unfavorable flexible-budget performance more
than offset it, leaving operating income $9,400 below the static
budget.

* Strategic and Behavioral Considerations

Flexible-budget analysis has important limitations. First, it assumes
linear cost behavior within the relevant range; at 12,000 units, Orion
may have exceeded the relevant range, causing step costs or capacity
constraints. Second, fixed costs are treated as fixed, but actual
fixed manufacturing overhead was $8,000 higher than budget. Third, the
analysis ignores quality, customer satisfaction, delivery performance,
scrap, rework, and employee morale. Higher volume may have been
achieved by sacrificing quality or by using expedited materials and
overtime (Kaplan & Norton, 2001). Fourth, standards may be outdated or
unrealistic. Fifth, aggregate variances do not separate price and
quantity effects, limiting diagnostic value.

Behaviorally, variance analysis can encourage dysfunctional decisions.
Managers may cut maintenance, reduce quality checks, or delay spending
to meet short-term budget targets (Merchant, 1985). Orion should
therefore use flexible budgets alongside nonfinancial measures and
strategic performance indicators (Kaplan & Norton, 2001). Budgeting
research also suggests that organizations benefit when budgets are
linked to strategy and when managers participate in the budgeting
process rather than being evaluated only on rigid variance outcomes
(Libby & Lindsay, 2010).

* Recommendations

1) *Adopt flexible-budget reporting for monthly performance
   evaluation.* The controller and FP&A team should present actual
   results against a flexible budget at actual volume, with activity
   variances separated from flexible-budget variances. This will
   prevent volume-driven cost increases from being misclassified as
   poor cost control.

2) *Investigate and correct unfavorable variable-cost variances.* The
   production manager should lead a review of direct materials, direct
   labor, and variable manufacturing overhead. Orion should identify
   whether the $42,000 unfavorable production-related variance comes
   from price, usage, efficiency, or standards. A measurable target is
   to return direct materials to $24 per unit, direct labor to $15 per
   unit, and variable manufacturing overhead to $6 per unit by the
   next quarter.

3) *Address sales price erosion.* The sales manager should analyze why
   the average selling price fell from $80 to $78. Possible causes
   include discounting, competitive pressure, or product mix. The goal
   should be to restore average selling price to $80 or formally
   revise the budget if market conditions have changed.

4) *Review fixed manufacturing overhead and capacity.* The plant
   manager should examine the $8,000 unfavorable fixed manufacturing
   overhead variance, including overtime, maintenance, and capacity
   utilization.  Fixed selling and administrative expenses were $3,000
   favorable, but management should confirm this was not achieved by
   deferring necessary spending.

5) *Add nonfinancial and strategic performance measures.* Orion should
   implement a balanced scorecard that includes on-time delivery,
   defect rates, customer satisfaction, scrap, rework, and safety
   (Kaplan & Norton, 2001). These measures will help ensure that
   volume growth does not damage quality or long-term customer
   relationships.

* Conclusion

Orion's quarter shows both opportunity and weakness. Sales volume
exceeded plan, and the activity-driven contribution margin variance
was $64,000 favorable. However, actual operating income fell below
both the static and flexible budgets. The static-budget-only view is
misleading, but the flexible budget reveals real operating problems:
unfavorable sales price, variable costs, and fixed manufacturing
overhead. The quarter should not be considered fully successful. Orion
should adopt flexible-budget reporting, investigate cost variances,
improve pricing discipline, and integrate nonfinancial measures into
performance evaluation.
